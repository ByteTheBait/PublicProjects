Milestone Working — Physics & Design Decisions

Milestone 1a — Linear Scale Calculation

The universe is 13.8 × 10⁹ yr = 4.354 × 10¹⁷ seconds. On a 1000-pixel screen, each pixel represents 4.354 × 10¹⁴ s. The first second would occupy:

1 s ÷ (4.354 × 10¹⁴ s/px) ≈ 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁵ pixels

That is roughly ten million times smaller than a single hydrogen atom. Completely invisible — you would need a screen approximately 1.4 × 10⁸ km wide (about the distance from Earth to the Sun) just to show the first second at 1 pixel per second.

Milestone 1b — Logarithmic Mapping Formula

Given time t in seconds, with t_min = 5.4 × 10⁻⁴³ s and t_max = 4.354 × 10¹⁷ s:

x_fraction = (log₁₀(t) − log₁₀(t_min)) / (log₁₀(t_max) − log₁₀(t_min))

This spans log₁₀(4.354 × 10¹⁷) − log₁₀(5.4 × 10⁻⁴³) ≈ 17.64 − (−42.27) = 59.9 decades. The CMB at 380,000 yr ≈ 1.2 × 10¹³ s maps to fraction ≈ 0.52 — just left of centre. The first second (1 s) maps to fraction ≈ 0.71 — which means the first second occupies 71% of the width, confirming the log scale gives the early universe its rightful real estate.

Milestone 1d — Why t_min = Planck Time

At scales smaller than the Planck time (5.4 × 10⁻⁴³ s), quantum gravitational fluctuations become so energetic that spacetime itself has no smooth, continuous structure. Neither general relativity nor quantum field theory applies; both break down. We have no physical theory capable of making predictions before this moment, so there is no meaningful physical starting point earlier than the Planck time.

Why a Logarithmic Scale?

  • On a linear scale, the entire epoch from the Big Bang to recombination (380,000 years) occupies 2.8 × 10⁻⁵ of the total timeline — invisible at any usable screen resolution.
  • The interesting physics spans 43 orders of magnitude in the first 380,000 years alone.
  • The log scale gives each factor-of-10 in time equal visual weight, matching the way physicists actually divide cosmic history into epochs.
  • A log scale makes it immediately legible that "the early universe" is not a thin sliver — it dominates the visual space, as it should.

Animation — Option A: Temperature Colour Slider

The slider maps cosmic time logarithmically to temperature (from 10³² K at the Planck epoch to 2.725 K today). The colour swatch shows the corresponding blackbody emission colour — the actual colour that region of space would glow if you could see it. At 10³² K: blinding white (all visible wavelengths). At 5000–6000 K: yellow-white (like the Sun's surface). At 3000 K: deep orange-red (the CMB release). Below ~1000 K: infrared and below — shown as crimson fading to black, because the light has left the visible spectrum entirely.

Design Decisions

  • Horizontal timeline — suits the left-to-right reading direction and makes the animation slider intuitive alongside the track.
  • Timeline ends at "Now" — the universe will continue but we have no confirmed physics for the far future; the timeline ends at what we know.
  • Planck epoch shown with a hard left boundary and a label — the edge is clear, not faded, because it is a real physical boundary of our knowledge, not a gradual uncertainty.
  • Colour palette — white-blue (hot) → orange → deep red → black (cold), mapping blackbody radiation across ~30 orders of magnitude.
