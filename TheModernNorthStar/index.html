The Modern North Star

From secret spirituals and coded newspapers to encrypted apps and satellite networks — the abolitionist spirit never stopped moving.

Scroll
"Right is of no sex — Truth is of no color — God is the Father of us all, and we are brethren." — Frederick Douglass, masthead of The North Star, Dec. 3, 1847

In 1847, a formerly enslaved man moved to Rochester, New York — the last stop on the Underground Railroad before Canada — and launched a newspaper named after the star that guided freedom-seekers north through the dark. He called it The North Star. Today, migrants fleeing persecution navigate by a different kind of star: encrypted signals, closed social media groups, and mutual-aid networks that span continents. The technology has changed. The moral stakes have not.

Oral / Musical Code

Spirituals & "Follow the Drinking Gourd"

Encoded hymns carried operational intelligence — when to leave, where to go, which rivers to cross. Harriet Tubman used owl calls as safety signals. Songs about "Canaan" meant the free North. Only trusted insiders knew the double meanings.

Signal & End-to-End Encryption

Activist groups use Signal to flag ICE enforcement sweeps in real time and coordinate rapid-response demonstrations. In Minneapolis, organizers built "Defrost MN," a crowd-sourced database of ICE vehicle plates, shared through encrypted channels.

Railroad Code Language

Passengers, Stations & Conductors

The Railroad used an entire operational vocabulary drawn from rail travel: freedom-seekers were "passengers" or "cargo," safe houses were "stations," helpers were "conductors" and "agents." The Ohio River became the "River Jordan."

Legal Tech Tools

TurnSignl & Know-Your-Rights Apps

Apps like TurnSignl connect people confronted by ICE almost instantly to an immigration attorney, and automatically upload video to cloud storage. The app, born from the 2020 racial justice movement, now has over 285,000 users navigating enforcement encounters.

Legal Access · Cloud Evidence · Instant Counsel
Celestial Navigation

The North Star (Polaris)

Escaping enslaved people navigated north by fixing on Polaris — found by following the outer stars of the Big Dipper. This literal guiding light became the metaphor for Douglass's newspaper: accessible truth as the star of hope for millions.

Part II · The Connection

"How Is Your Work a
Continuation of the Abolitionist Spirit?"

When Douglass launched The North Star, its masthead proclaimed that truth transcends color and sex — a radical universalism in 1847. The newspaper's purpose was not merely to report on slavery but to make the enslaved visible, to give them a platform, and to build a network of people who could act.

Q: How is your work a continuation of the abolitionist spirit?
"Abolitionists understood that you cannot simply denounce injustice — you have to build the infrastructure of freedom. Safe houses, communication networks, legal support, documentation. We do the same work, with different tools."
— Representative, immigrant legal aid network (composite of public mission statements from RAICES, Immigrant Legal Resource Center, and National Immigration Law Center)

Modern migrant support organizations draw directly from this playbook. Rapid-response legal networks give migrants immediate access to counsel — the equivalent of the Underground Railroad's "station masters" who knew the law and could offer protection. Encrypted networks provide operational security that mirrors the coded spirituals: only trusted insiders understand the full meaning of what is being communicated.

The parallel extends to risk. Underground Railroad participants could be prosecuted under the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850. Today, advocates who assist undocumented people face potential federal charges. The threat of legal punishment for offering humanitarian aid is not a new invention — it is a recurring feature of every era in which the law and human dignity have come into conflict.

On the dual nature of technology
"The same digital traces that let us organize and help people also leave them vulnerable to surveillance. We live with that tension every day. Frederick Douglass lived with it too — every issue of The North Star was evidence that could be used against him."
— Synthesized from public statements by Migration Policy Institute researchers, 2024

What unifies all of these efforts across 180 years is the same conviction that animated Douglass: that the condition of being a human being in flight from persecution is a moral claim upon those who are not. The technology changes. The direction — north, toward safety — stays the same.

Part III · The Parallels

Same Spirit, New Tools

1847 ↔ 2019

The North Star → Reborn

Douglass's newspaper was relaunched in 2019 with the Douglass family's blessing, explicitly naming the goal of carrying forward abolitionist journalism for a new era of civil rights struggles.

Print → Digital Media
1850 ↔ 2025

Fugitive Slave Act ↔ Enforcement Raids

Both eras feature federal laws that criminalize assistance to people fleeing persecution, and both eras have produced organized networks of citizens willing to break those laws on moral grounds.

Legal Risk → Moral Imperative
1850s ↔ Now

Underground Railroad station masters provided shelter and legal knowledge. Today's immigrant legal aid organizations provide counsel, documentation, and sanctuary — the same function at scale.

Dual-Use Communication

Spirituals appeared innocent to oppressors while carrying operational meaning to freedom-seekers. Encrypted apps similarly allow organizers to communicate freely while maintaining security from authorities.

Celestial → Satellite
